From reports nobody had time to read to one sales dashboard

Management wanted to know what the sales team was doing without asking for it. I started with a daily report, then a weekly one, then a Slack bot that captured standups, and finally replaced all three with a single dashboard. The version here runs on sample data, with fictional names and numbers.

Why I built it

The sales team worked in the CRM and posted standups in Slack, but nobody could see the whole picture without asking each person. Management reviews ran on a long Word document that took days to fill in, and by the time it was read, the numbers were out of date.

The goal I set was simple: every figure in a review should come straight from the CRM, with no one retyping it, and anyone should be able to check it for any date range.

How it evolved

Each step solved the problem of the one before it, and each one showed what was still missing.

  1. 1. Daily reportWhat happened today
  2. 2. Weekly reportWhat changed this week
  3. 3. Slack botWhat people said they did
  4. 4. Sales dashboardAll of it, for any period
  1. Step 1Daily report

    Make each day's activity visible A CRM-driven report to the sales manager, every weekday evening

    • What it did: pulled each salesperson's calls, meetings, notes and deal changes from Freshsales through its API and produced one PDF, so nobody had to fill in a sheet.
    • Rules I had to settle: a call counts only when the contact's attempt counter rises on that day; call notes on deals count too; old data loaded late is marked as backfilled, not counted as today's work.
    • What it taught me: a day is too short a window to judge anyone, and the report said what happened but not why.
  2. Step 2Weekly report

    Compare weeks, not days One team report each Saturday morning

    • What it did: summed the daily figures per person and added a team summary page, with a section per salesperson showing deals, meetings and a day-by-day log.
    • Design choice: no weekly targets. Each week is compared with last week and the four-week average, so the report shows direction instead of pass or fail.
    • What it taught me: the numbers needed context, and a PDF cannot answer the follow-up question a manager asks next.
  3. Step 3Slack bot

    Capture what people say they are doing A bot that collects standups so the story sits beside the numbers

    • What it did: prompted the team in Slack morning and evening, filed morning posts as plans and evening posts as updates, and kept them in one log.
    • What went wrong: scheduled runs fired late and posts were split at the wrong time, so updates were filed as plans. I fixed the cut-off at a fixed hour and made the bot skip a prompt when someone had already posted.
    • What it taught me: the CRM shows what was recorded and Slack shows what was meant, and a reviewer needs both in one place.
  4. Step 4Sales dashboard

    Put it all in one page that works for any date range The version you can open below

    • What it does: one self-contained page with an Overview, a Meetings tab and one tab per region. Pick any period and every number, chart and table recalculates in the browser from dated CRM snapshots.
    • Why one page: management reads it without opening the CRM, a click on any figure lists the records behind it, and there is nothing to send or attach.
    • What it replaced: the long Word review, the daily PDF and most of the weekly PDF.

Decisions that shaped it

DecisionWhy
Everything comes from the CRM, read-onlyOne source of truth, no double entry, and the reports can never change a record.
Count by dated events, not by current stateA deal's stage today says nothing about last week. Dated events and snapshots let any period be rebuilt.
Define each metric once and write it downCalls, connected calls and meetings each had one rule. Arguments about the numbers became arguments about the rule.
Click any figure to see the recordsA number nobody can trace gets ignored. A list of the underlying deals gets checked, and then trusted.
One tab per region, same layoutManagers learn one page and then read all of them.
Single HTML fileIt opens from an email or a link, needs no server, and is easy to hand over.

About this demo

The real dashboard holds company data, so it cannot be public. I rebuilt it with the same engine and layout and fed it a generated history instead: ten fictional salespeople, nine regions (North, South, West and East zones, plus USA, Canada, Australia, Europe and the rest of the world), and about four months of invented activity. Every name, company and figure is fictional and the page says so.

Try a different date range, switch regions, filter by owner, or click a figure to see the deals behind it. The generator and the build script are in the repository.
